Lighthouse Attention

A training-only sparse attention trick that lets long-context transformers learn cheaply — then quietly steps aside so the model walks out as a normal dense-attention model.

Bowen Peng*, Subho Ghosh*, Jeffrey Quesnelle — Nous Research  ·  May 2026

Long-context training Sparse attention 𝒪(N log N) attention path Pre-training method

The one-paragraph version

Attention costs 𝒪(N²). That wall is what makes 128K- or 1M-token training brutal. Lighthouse Attention wraps a clever pre/post-processing layer around a regular FlashAttention call: it builds a small pyramid of pooled tokens, scores each entry by a parameter-free L2 norm, keeps only the top-K, runs attention on that tiny gathered sub-sequence, and scatters the answers back. After most of training, you remove the wrapper and run a short recovery phase with full dense attention. The result: a dense model, trained at long context, at roughly 𝒪(N log N) cost.

01 — Why this paper existsThe quadratic wall

Every token in self-attention compares itself to every other token. Double the context, quadruple the work. At 128K tokens attention is painful; at 1M it's most of the compute bill. Long-context capability is what makes today's frontier models do agentic reasoning, ingest long documents, and digest interleaved multimodal inputs — so the cost of training them at extreme sequence lengths matters.

There are already plenty of sparse attention ideas — MoBA, NSA, DSA, HISA, InfLLM — but most of them carry three baggage items:

  • Asymmetry. They pool keys and values but keep queries full-resolution. The "hierarchy" is just compressed memory, not a real multi-scale view.
  • Custom kernels. Selection is welded inside the attention kernel, so they can't reuse stock FlashAttention.
  • The recoverability question. If you train under sparse attention, does the model still know how to do dense attention at inference?

Lighthouse Attention tackles all three at once.

02 — The lighthouse metaphorDon't light the whole ocean

A lighthouse doesn't illuminate every wave. It chooses where to point.

That's the intuition. Instead of letting every token attend to every other token, Lighthouse picks a small set of important regions at multiple zoom levels and only does the expensive computation on those. The rest of the sequence is summarized cheaply and the summaries — not the raw tokens — get a vote.

03 — Where the novelty actually livesThree concrete contributions

i

Symmetric pyramid pooling of Q, K, and V

Earlier sparse methods only pool keys and values. Lighthouse pools queries too, at the same resolutions. A pooled query and a pooled key live in the same space, so they can be compared meaningfully. The result is a real multi-scale representation, not just compressed memory.

ii

Selection lives outside the attention kernel

Top-K selection is done by a custom chunked-bitonic kernel, then the chosen entries are gathered into a small dense sub-sequence and fed to stock FlashAttention. No bespoke sparse-attention kernel to maintain. Scatter-back distributes outputs to all the base positions each pooled entry represents.

iii

Two-stage training: train cheap, recover dense

Pre-train most of the budget under Lighthouse. Run a short recovery phase with full dense SDPA at the end. The model that walks out is a normal dense-attention model — competitive with (or better than) one trained dense from scratch on the same token budget.

04 — The algorithmFour stages, click through them

Lighthouse replaces SDPA with a four-stage pipeline. The middle stage is just stock FlashAttention on a small dense input — the cleverness is in the wrapping.

STAGE 01 · LINEAR COST

Pool Q, K, V into an L-level pyramid

Average-pool the projections by a factor p at each level. Level 0 is the full sequence; level 1 averages every p tokens; level 2 averages every p²; and so on. Each pyramid entry holds a coherent triple (Q, K, V) — pooled together so they stay aligned in the same representation space.

Q_i⁽ℓ⁾ = mean( Q_j : j ∈ window_i⁽ℓ⁾ )
K_i⁽ℓ⁾ = mean( K_j : j ∈ window_i⁽ℓ⁾ )
V_i⁽ℓ⁾ = mean( V_j : j ∈ window_i⁽ℓ⁾ )

07 — Figure 2 from the paperHow the selector actually picks

The selector is parameter-free. There's no learned scorer network — just L2 norms of Q and K, max-pooled up the pyramid. Coarse-level scores reuse the finer-level norms, so the descent into the pyramid is implicit: children of non-parents are never expanded.

Figure 2 · from the paper
Pyramid Pool and the Hierarchical Selector. (1) Pyramid Pool mean-pools Q, K, V by p^ℓ; lines show which tokens feed each summary. (2) Norm Score computes parameter-free L2 norms ‖Q⁽ℓ⁾‖₂, ‖K⁽ℓ⁾‖₂ — coarser levels reuse finer norms via max-pool. (3) Chunked Bitonic Top-K keeps top-K parents (dark) that descend, while rejected-kept entries (light) emit straight to ℐ. Pruning is implicit: children of non-parents are never expanded.

09 — Why dense recovery is the key trickTwo-stage training

This is the bit that makes Lighthouse a training method rather than yet another inference-time hack.

StageAttention pathDurationGoal
Pre-trainingLighthouse (subquadratic)Majority of trainingCheap long-context exposure
RecoveryFull dense SDPAShort tailRe-acclimate to dense attention

The central correctness claim: after the recovery tail, the model performs at least as well on dense-attention metrics as a model trained dense-from-scratch on the same token budget. The hierarchical training signal "doesn't hollow out the model's ability to use full attention at inference."

Why does this work? Gradients never flow through the selector. The top-K op is discrete; the paper makes no attempt to differentiate through it (no straight-through estimator, no auxiliary loss). The model only learns to make its Q, K, V projections useful given the parameter-free norm-based selection. When the wrapper is removed, the projections are still ordinary, well-behaved projections — they just had restricted training contexts.

11 — Where this mattersApplications

  • Frontier long-context pretraining. 128K → 1M → 10M-token training without the quadratic blow-up. The paper's stated motivation is the long-context arms race around GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, Llama 3, Qwen, DeepSeek-V3, Kimi K1.5.
  • Drop-in dense inference. Because the recovered model is just a dense transformer, you ship the standard FlashAttention path. No custom sparse kernel at serving time — unlike NSA, MoBA, DSA, HISA.
  • Engineering simplicity. Selection lives outside the attention kernel. The team that maintains your training stack doesn't have to babysit a bespoke sparse-attention kernel for every new GPU.
  • Agentic / multimodal workloads. The downstream beneficiaries are long-chain agentic reasoning, long-document understanding, and interleaved multimodal sequences — all of the things that need long context but don't want to pay full quadratic price during training.

12 — Mental shortcutsFive intuitions worth keeping

𝟙

Symmetric pooling matters

Pooling Q and K together keeps them in the same space — that's what lets coarse and fine entries compete on a single ranking. Asymmetric methods can't do this honestly.

𝟚

Norm-as-importance is parameter-free

Large-magnitude Q/K vectors carry more weight in softmax anyway. Using ‖·‖₂ as a score is cheap, stable, and trains nothing.

𝟛

Selection outside the kernel = reuse FlashAttention

By the time we call attention, the input is small and dense. Stock FlashAttention can just run. No custom CUDA per sparsity pattern.

𝟜

No gradient through top-K

Discrete selection is left alone. Projections learn to be useful when picked — not to learn a learned picker. This is unusually clean.

𝟝

Recovery answers the killer question

"Did the sparse training corrupt the model for dense use?" The two-stage recipe is the empirical answer — short dense recovery, full-attention competence.

13 — Honest caveatsLimitations to keep in mind

  • The paper itself frames its experiments as preliminary small-scale LLM pre-training runs. We don't yet have frontier-scale numbers.
  • Because top-K is non-differentiable and there's no learned scorer, the model can't directly shape what gets selected — only how to be useful given the norm-based selection.
  • Sequence length must satisfy a divisibility constraint: p^(L−1) divides N.
  • The recovery phase adds an extra training step. The full-trajectory savings come from the bulk pre-training being much cheaper.
